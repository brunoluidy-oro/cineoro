# Acting task — full method

Read this before writing any ACTING block. These definitions come from Tigran's directing school
(carried over from the `cinedance` skill) and from the acting method proven on a 20-minute AI short.
Apply them exactly as written; do not replace them with textbook Stanislavski summaries.

⚠️ **Goal / Obstacle / Tactic here are PERFORMANCE terms** — what the actor does to the partner,
moment to moment. `cineoro-story` uses the same three words as DRAMATURGY terms (what the story does,
to decide coverage). Keep the two lanes separate.

**Contents:** core principle · the ladder · writing the block · contrast pairing · worked examples ·
animals and processes · common failures

---

## Core principle

An acting task is when the character is **INVESTED in his/her tactic of reaching the goal.**

- It is NOT a description of external behaviour ("eyes flick", "brows lift", "he looks sad") — that
  is playing the result, and it produces dead faces.
- It is NOT necessarily physical action. The tactic can live entirely **in the eyes**: a different
  look when someone humiliates vs. begs; a person arguing from conviction constantly checks BOTH eyes
  of the partner, hunting for a sparkle of trust, registering whether the partner is interested or
  drifting — and adjusts to what he finds.
- The eye movement IS the doing. Aliveness = the mind visibly working on the task, moment to moment.
- Never fix dead eyes with lighting tricks (catchlights etc.). Fix them by giving the eyes a task.

The full ladder, top to bottom:
**super-objective (the character's whole-film want) → the scene's event → motive → the goal of this
moment → the obstacle → the tactic, a concrete playable action.**

## The ladder (work strictly in this order)

### 0. Read the WHOLE scene dialogue first
Never build a task from one line. The task lives across the entire exchange — where it holds, where
it breaks. Check every line of every character before directing any of them.

### 1. Goal of the SCENE — one shared direction
The scene has ONE goal: a single direction ALL characters play toward, usually unspoken — a mutual
silent agreement about how this time will be lived.
Example (mother packs son's suitcase, army in an hour): **make it painless — hide the feeling, don't
leave each other with pain or sorrow, never lose the positiveness.** It belongs to both at once.
The scene goal is NOT the film's dramaturgic function (the reveal, the theme). Characters never know
or play the film's purposes — those are accomplished THROUGH them, as a byproduct.

### 1a. The ENDING names the event
Always look at how the scene ENDS before naming its event. The last line/beat is the key you read
the whole scene backward through. Watch for the double-meaning last line — spoken about one thing,
meant about another ("Poor bastard. Just can't forgive himself" — said looking at the patient, meant
about himself).

### 1b. ALL in one event
The event must contain EVERY character in the scene — including silent or unconscious ones — as
participants or mirrors of the same process. If a character stands outside the named event, the
event is named wrong.
Approved example — event = **"the search for self-forgiveness"**: two compromised men over a third
who is literally wired into his own unforgivable day. The patient can't forgive himself in the
dream; the medic can't forgive himself at the bedside (his care for the patient is his penance); the
doctor stopped trying long ago (his "carelessness" is post-hope scar tissue — he watches the young
medic, curious whether THIS one finds the way out he never found). Three mirrors of one sentence.
The surface — "routine rounds" — is only the terrain the event moves through, not the event.

### 1c. The PHYSICAL ACTION is the channel
The surface activity of the scene (the "terrain" — routine rounds; packing a suitcase; rigging a
sled) stays as the PHYSICAL ACTION, and each character pursues the event THROUGH it — each via his
own distinct, visible physical behaviour. The invisible task must have a physical channel the
camera can read.
- **Patient** — through REMEMBERING: REM under closed lids, the tear.
- **Doctor** — through OBLIGATED ACTIONS DONE RIGHT: writing correct metrics, on time, precisely,
  double-checking an entry — flawless procedure is what remains of his doctorhood.
- **Medic** — through CARING: gestures beyond the checklist (checking the man, not the chart).
Rule: give every character his own physical channel for the same event — different behaviours, one
event, one terrain.

### 2. Each character's MOTIVE — different fuel, same direction
Every character pushes along the same scene direction, but each **for his own reason**. The son
keeps it painless *for mom*; the mother *out of superstition* (no tears before a journey). Same
vector, different fuel — the fuel makes each performance distinct while the scene reads unified.
Motives can be alternative hypotheses; the director picks. Given circumstances constrain motives: a
man who already took a compromising job cannot play a moral innocent.

### 3. Each character's GOAL — the personal fight
Born from the motive: what this person is fighting for himself inside the scene. Ordinary,
personal, playable. Never the same words as the scene goal, never the theme.

### 4. OBSTACLE — what presses against the direction
The thing threatening to break the scene's line (the real feeling pressing to surface; the case too
horrifying to stay routine). One crack and the shared goal collapses. This pressure is what the
audience actually feels — precisely because nobody plays it; everyone plays keeping it out.

### 5. TACTIC — the acting task proper
The invested, moment-to-moment pursuit, written as what the character is DOING to the partner — with
the eye-work named as purposeful action:
- checking both of the partner's eyes for a sparkle of trust
- registering after each point: did it land? interested or about to smirk?
- stealing looks and snapping back before being caught
- measuring the partner, memorizing him, comparing what I feel with what he shows
Beats are keyed to the actual dialogue words **and to the seconds of the timeline**. Where the
script demands it, mark the point where a character's fuel runs out and the line breaks (the
medic's "...poor bastard," under his breath — the break IS the delivery of the scene).

**"Plays holding still."** A powerful special case: the character's tactic is to hold absolutely
still so the partner gets what he needs. She does not play grief; she plays holding still. The grief
shows only because holding still costs her so much.

## Writing the block

```
ACTING — [NAME] (fully invested in the tactic; the work happens in the eyes)
SCENE DIRECTION (shared, unspoken): [one line]
EVENT: [named from the ending]
MOTIVE (fuel): [why HE pushes that direction]
GOAL: [his personal fight]
OBSTACLE: [what presses against the line, what one crack costs]
TACTIC: [what he does to the partner, with the eye-work as action]
MOMENT TO MOMENT:
— [s–s] "[dialogue words]" — [verb at the partner + what the eyes check]
— [s–s] "[dialogue words]" — [verb + eye-work]
— [where the line breaks, if it breaks]
LIVING EYES: [physiology line + one involuntary detail]
NOT: [wrong performances]
```

Rules:
- Verbs directed at the partner; no adjectives of emotion as instruction ("sadly", "nervously").
- No emotional facial choreography ("brows lift", "mouth trembles"). Physiology (saccades, blink
  cadence, asymmetry) belongs to LIVING EYES and is allowed.
- Nobody plays the emotion; everyone plays the direction.
- Every character in frame gets living eyes this way — including silent listeners: a listener's task
  is also real ("decide if he's serious", "wait for the punchline", "protect the mood").

## Contrast pairing (build duos on mirrored +/−)

A two-character scene is richer when the pair is built on CONTRAST — each character carries a plus
and a minus, mirrored against the other, with one axis being the essential one the audience reads.

Approved example — the lab pair:
- **DOCTOR — Corporate Loyal (+) / CARELESS (−):** genuinely loyal — but to the corporation and the
  money, not to patients. On the essential axis — care for the patient — he is MINUS.
- **MEDIC — Compromised (−) / CARING (+):** compromised by the money (he took the job, he knows what
  this place is) — but caring toward the patient. On the essential axis he is PLUS.

Rules:
- Each character gets one + and one −, inverted relative to the partner.
- Name the ESSENTIAL AXIS of the scene — that opposition is what the audience reads.
- Both still push the same scene direction — the contrast lives UNDER it and leaks out through the
  tactics and the eyes: the careless one's routine is real; the caring one's routine is armour, and
  it cracks.
- The seeming trait is often scar tissue over its opposite: "careless" = hope lost, not care absent.
  Direct the history, not the surface.

## Worked examples (approved)

**Mother/son suitcase.** Scene direction: part without pain, stay positive. Her motive:
superstition. His: for mom. Her goal: send him off strong. His: leave her calm, show he's ready.
Obstacle: the real feeling, pressing every second. Tactic: she packs "ordinarily", eyes stealing to
him, measuring, memorizing, snapping back to the socks the moment he looks up; he eats "casually",
watching her hands, not her face — because her face is where the danger is. The audience cries
precisely because nobody on screen does.

**Father who mistakes his daughter for his dead wife.** His goal: reach his wife, whom he has just
found. Obstacle: his body barely works. Tactic: touch her face to make sure she is real — he is not
confused in this moment, he is certain, and that certainty is what makes it terrible. Her goal: keep
him calm so he will drink and sleep. Obstacle: to do that she has to let him have her dead mother.
Tactic: hold absolutely still and answer in her mother's register.

**Daughter tethering an absent father to a sled.** Her tactic: make him physically impossible to
lose and get moving again inside a minute; the tie is rigging, the walk is transit. Not looking at
his face is part of the tactic, because looking would cost her something she cannot spend. His
tactic: none — he stands where he was put and walks because something pulls him. The event: the
father has been made fast to the load like gear, and he does not object.

**Madness played as tactics.** An absent man whose body keeps doing what a hunter's life taught it:
he ties good knots, he predicts weather correctly, he says "not that way" — and he is right. Madness
is never "acting crazy"; it is a series of competent actions in the wrong world.

## Animals and physical processes

Life in the eyes works for animals and processes too. An animal's tactic is concrete: a trapped
animal's tactic is to fight while it still can; a working dog's is to hold its line and watch the
handler; a cat's is to keep the exit in view. A process has a "tactic" too: fire fights to catch and
the hands protect the ember; a rope resists and the hands force it.

## Common failures to catch

- Task built from one line instead of the whole dialogue → re-read the scene first.
- The film's reveal/theme assigned to a character as his task → characters never play the film's
  purposes.
- Scene goal confused with character goal → scene goal is shared; character goals are personal.
- Motive ignores given circumstances → re-derive the motive.
- Prescribed emotional face choreography instead of eye-work as purposeful action → rewrite as verbs.
- Emotion adjectives as direction → replace with the task that produces the emotion.
- Listener with no task → the reaction shot will be dead.

## Pre-generation checklist

Is the tactic an active verb, not an adjective? Is there a small involuntary detail? Does the
obstacle explain *why* the face looks like that? Could a real actor play this?
