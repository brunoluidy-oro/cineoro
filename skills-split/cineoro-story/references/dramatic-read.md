# Dramatic read — deciding coverage, not rewriting

Run silently whenever the input is a script or a written beat. Never shown in deliverables unless the
STOP rule fires. Origin: the `cinedance` dramatic read; definitions kept exactly.

⚠️ **These five terms are DRAMATURGY definitions.** They are not the acting-task definitions in
`cineoro-performance`, which use Goal / Obstacle / Tactic for what the actor does to the partner
moment to moment. This file decides *how many shots and where they cut*; the acting task decides *what
the eyes do inside them*.

---

## The five elements

**1. Goal.** The single unchanging thing the hero is fighting for: to fix what has already been
established and reach the resolving result as fast as possible. Every scene goal is a causal link
toward the story goal.

**2. Obstacle.** A strong circumstance that *jeopardizes* a stage of the path or the whole goal — it
threatens, it does not merely cost. Note its scale: *local* (threatens this stage) or *global*
(threatens the goal).

**3. Tactic.** The move the threat forces — a reasonable guess on the hero's current, often incomplete
knowledge. Each failed tactic returns information that narrows the search and reshapes the next move.

**4. Reversal.** A turn against expectation: my own action flips on me, or hidden agency is revealed,
or the situation turns opposite to what the tactic intended. At least one per resolved sequence.

**5. Value Shift.** The change in the *audience's* verdict on a character, triggered by a reversal. A
reversal with no value shift is inert.

## The read → coverage mapping

| Element | What it decides |
|---|---|
| **Goal** | What the scene must deliver. A thin causal link earns fewer shots; a stage of the path earns more. |
| **Obstacle** | Where the pressure sits. Pressure is played by *holding*, not cutting — fewer, longer shots through it. |
| **Tactic** | Where a character changes approach. **A tactic switch is the motivated cut.** |
| **Reversal** | The turn. A **sustained shot of its own** — not split across generations, not undercut by extra cuts. If it lands on a face, that is the close-up the scene was built toward. |
| **Value Shift** | Where the scene lands. The final shot holds long enough for the audience to arrive at the new verdict. Ending one beat early is the most common way a shotlist kills a scene. |

Practical consequences:
- Split on tactic changes and the reversal, not on clock arithmetic. Thirty seconds is a generation's
  capacity, not its logic.
- The reversal never splits across two generations.
- Escalation without reversal is one continuous build — long held shots, few cuts.
- Silent characters are load-bearing at the reversal — the value shift often lands on the listener.

## Where this meets the acting task

The reversal is where a character's fuel runs out. The read says *at which beat* the line breaks; the
acting task says *what the eyes are doing when it breaks*. Write them as one decision: the shot that
carries the reversal gets the sustained framing *and* the ACTING block that marks the break in its
moment-to-moment beats.

## STOP rule — never fix the script

If the read comes back broken — no obstacle with anything at risk, a tactic that returns zero
information, a sequence resolving with no reversal, or a reversal with no before/after verdict — do
not repair it. Deliver the coverage as the script reads and add one line in chat naming the scene and
what the read couldn't find:

> "Scene 6 resolves without a reversal, so its coverage is one continuous build — nothing for a turn to
> land on. Worth a look before we shoot it."

Fixing structure is the `tig-scene-engine` skill's job, and the user's call.
